XXI LES FUNERAILLES DE L'AMOUR
      
Les cornettes d'oubli sonnent sur les murailles.
N'allumez plus les feux! Dispersez les batailles!
Apaisez sous le chant la légende et le dit!
Voici venir la fin de notre longue histoire
Quelle barque a cinglé des rives de mémoire?
Quelle procession aux îles resplendit?

Obsédantes, des voix prolongent des silences.
L'ombre n'abdique pas quand renonce la danse.
Les couples dédaignés errent au fil des nuits.
Ils mènent leurs amours et s'enivrent en rêve.
Le maître ne connaît la mine qui le grève
Ni pourquoi ses regrets s'enténèbrent d'ennuis.

Dix ans durant, les rois muselèrent les portes.
Ils élevèrent haut des citadelles fortes,
Les clairons ont clamé pour étouffer le lai.
Mais rien ne durera, hors Pâris et Hélène.
Troie est tombé rongé par une cantilène
Pour que brille longtemps le conte qu'il celait.

Apprêtez à l'amour de dignes funérailles.
Mais si les blés sont hauts, sourdes sont les semailles.
Aux confins de mon âme, enfouissez le mort -
Et qu'il ne reste plus un seul débris du songe!
Amour est un miroir au reflet de mensonge.
Le squelette capté monte, Gorgone d'or.

L'aube qui va lever chassera les étoiles.
Un ivrogne en chantant trébuche sur des dalles.
Ce chant m'est trop connu dont je sais les accords.
Amoncelez la nuit, le rocher, le silence,
Et que tout ce tombeau ma hantise balance
S'il peut être un tombeau lorsqu'il n'est pas de corps.

Michel Galiana (c) 1991




XXI THE FUNERAL OF LOVE
      
The horns of oblivion ring along the rampart.
No more fires be lit!  Let the host fall apart!
Let legend and tale be soothed beneath the song!
Behold how to an end comes our long story!
What boat has sailed from the shores of memory?
What is, on the islands, this train gleaming along?

Obsessive voices prolonging the silences.
As shadow does not yield to receding dances.
Disparaged couples still wander through the nights.
They carry on their loves and they grow drunk in dreams.
The master does not know the sap that ruins him,
His regrets grow darker with spleen: he wonders why.

For ten long years the kings strove to muzzle the gates.
They erected mighty citadels to great heights.
Trumpets were made to blare. The lay must not be heard.
But nothing will endure, save Paris and Helen.
Troy did fall, undermined by a simple poem,
So that the tale it hid might shine forevermore.

For love let us prepare a worthy interment
But when the wheat stands tall, the sowing is silent.
In the outermost fields of my soul, be its tomb! 
And of the dream we dreamt the slightest trace must go!
For love is a mirror with deceptive shadow:
The skeleton it raised is a golden Gorgon.

The dawn about to rise will chase away the stars.
A bawling drunkard has stumbled over the tar
Of this familiar song I could sing all the chords.
Pile up the night, the rock, the silence to a heap
So that out of that tomb my haunting may not creep—
If it can be a tomb where never was a corpse.
Transl. Christian Souchon 01.01.2005-2026 (c) (r) All rights reserved

Note :

Le dernier vers résume le contenu tragique du poème. Ce n'est pas la perte de l'être aimé qu'il déplore, mais celle de l'amour qu'on éprouvait pour lui.

The last line sums up the tragic content of this poem. This is not a lament about the beloved one but about the fact that the lover has ceased to love.

 Les funérailles de l’amour is one of Michel Galiana’s most ambitious poems — a long, architectonic meditation on the end of passion, the burial of desire, and the strange afterlife of memory. It is not a lament but a ritual: a funeral rite for love itself. The poem’s tone is solemn, visionary, and deliberately ambiguous, oscillating between myth, dream, and metaphysical allegory. It belongs to the same emotional constellation as Lourde and Fini, but it precedes them chronologically and conceptually: it is the origin of the heaviness and cessation that dominate the 1991 sequence.

1. A funeral without a corpse
The poem’s paradox is announced early: these are the “funerals of love,” yet love has no body. What is buried is not a person but an experience — a constellation of memories, obsessions, and inner storms. The funeral is symbolic, not literal. Galiana stages the burial of a passion that has exhausted itself, leaving behind only echoes, shadows, and the “horns of oblivion.”
This absence of a corpse is crucial: the poem is not about death but about the impossibility of continuing.

2. The architecture of the poem: ramparts, fires, hosts, boats
Galiana constructs a vast allegorical landscape: ramparts, extinguished fires, a host dissolving, a boat departing from the shores of memory, obsessive voices, disparaged couples wandering through nights, kings erecting citadels, trumpets meant to silence the lay, Paris and Helen, Troy undermined by a poem.
These images form a mythic geography of loss. The poem is not autobiographical in a direct sense; it is mythologized autobiography. The young girl from Saint Cloud — whose shadow haunts Lourde — is transmuted here into Helen, into the lay that undermines Troy, into the poem that destroys citadels. Love becomes a force of ruin and revelation.

3. The kings who try to silence the lay
One of the poem’s most striking passages describes kings who attempt to “muzzle the gates” and prevent the lay from being heard. This is a metaphor for repression — the attempt to silence memory, desire, or obsession. But the lay survives. The poem insists that nothing endures except the myth: Paris and Helen, the poem that undermines Troy, the story that shines forevermore.
Galiana suggests that passion cannot be erased by willpower or authority; it must be buried through ritual.

4. The burial of love as agricultural metaphor
“But when the wheat stands tall, the sowing is silent.”
This line introduces a pastoral metaphor: love is buried like a seed. The burial is not annihilation but transformation. The “outermost fields of my soul” become the tomb of love. The poem’s agricultural imagery echoes the moorlands and pastures of Rebrousse temps: the inner landscape is rural, windswept, and fertile.

5. The Gorgon and the deceptive mirror
“For love is a mirror with deceptive shadow:
The skeleton it raised is a golden Gorgon.”
This is one of Galiana’s most powerful metaphors. Love is a mirror that distorts; its shadow is deceptive. The “skeleton” of love — what remains after passion dies — is a Gorgon: beautiful, terrifying, petrifying. The golden Gorgon is the memory that freezes the heart, the obsession that immobilizes time. This image anticipates the “closed star” of Lourde and the “empty square at noon” of Fini.

6. The drunkard and the familiar song
The poem ends with a drunkard stumbling over the tar of a familiar song. This figure is both comic and tragic: he represents the persistence of memory in degraded form. Even after the funeral, the song remains — but it is no longer exalted; it is banal, familiar, almost ridiculous. The poet wishes to pile up night, rock, and silence to prevent the haunting from escaping its tomb.
This is the emotional pivot: the funeral is necessary because memory continues to intrude.

7. A precursor to Lourde and Fini
Les funérailles de l’amour is the conceptual precursor to Lourde and Fini. The “distant weight of dreams and bones” in Lourde is the residue of this funeral. The “secret song” in Fini is the lay that kings tried to silence. The “peace” at the end of Fini is the peace sought at the end of Les funérailles de l’amour — the peace of a tomb without a corpse.

8. A ritual of closure
The poem is not a lament but a rite. It stages the burial of passion in order to free the poet from its haunting. But the burial is incomplete: the Gorgon remains, the drunkard sings, the lay survives. The poem ends with a question: can one bury love if love has no body? Galiana’s answer is ambiguous: one can bury the story, but not the echo.

Synthesis
Les Funérailles de l’amour is a mythic, allegorical, and metaphysical funeral for a passion that has exhausted itself. It is the origin of the heaviness, silence, and cessation that dominate the 1991 cycle. The poem stages the burial of love through ramparts, citadels, agricultural metaphors, and mythic figures. It ends not with closure but with haunting — a familiar song that refuses to die. The poem is both a tomb and a testament: a ritual of ending that cannot fully end.
