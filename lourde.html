IX LOURDE

...Ni le roulis. Le cri s'agence. S'achève
Le poids lointain de rêves et d'os. Relève
D'un nouveau poids. Soleil ni vent. Astre clos.
(Suprême amour, as-tu connu ton repos?)
Sur l'épervier s'est refermé le scandale.
Ni le roulis, ni le dépit.
		Plomb et dalle.

16 août 1991

IX HEAVY SLAB

... Nor rolling. Just a cry that takes shape. Abolished
The distant weight of dreams and bones. Replenished
By a new weight. No sun, no wind. A closed star.
(Supreme love, tell me, have you ever known your rest?) 
Upon the cast net has closed now the outrage 
No rolling, no bitterness:
				Heavy leaden slab.

August 16th 1991
Transl. Christian Souchon 01.04.2006 -2026 (c) (r) All rights reserved

Note :

Michel était friand de jeux de mots et de doubles-dens. L'"épervier" du 5ème vers est sans doute un filet de pêche qu'on jette et qui se referme, mais peut-être aussi un oiseau de proie pris dans un piège qui lui aussi se referme. J'imagine qu'avec cette image, il s'agit toujours et encore, trente-sept ans après, de la jeune fille de Saint-Cloud et de la phantasmagorie morbide ("le poids lointain de rêves et os") que ce souvenir charrie.
Entre temps cependant la plaie est cicatrisée et le cri de douleur ne sort plus spontanément: il "s'agence". Ce recours obligé aux ruses de l'homme de lettres (hendécasyllabes, phrases inachevées ou incomplètes, style pseudo-télégraphique...) est, tout comme l'ancienne obsession dont il prend la "relève", une pesante servitude "un nouveau poids". C'est là le "scandale" qui se referme sur l'épervier. Le responsable est le temps qui fige et calme et fait du "soleil un astre clos". Une "lourde" chape de "plomb" qui vient à bout de tout.
Pourquoi ce titre "Lourde" au féminin? Y a-t-il là un nouveau jeu de mots et faut-il lire "lourdé", un mot d'argot pour dire "Mis à la porte!"
A l'évidence, il faut rapprocher ce texte d'un autre poème de 1990: Les funérailles de l'amour.

Ma nouvelle amie, l'intelligence artificielle, m'a permis de corriger une erreur grave dans ma traduction anglaise: j'avais traduit "le cri s'agence" par "The cry comes forth", ce qui ôtait tout sens à ce poème.
Je lui ai soumis le commentaire ci-dessus. Après m'avoir gratifié, comme à son habitude, d'éloges dithyrambiques, elle m'a signalé qu'on pouvait faire une lecture moins autobiographique et plus générale de ce poème où chacun des 50 mots compte.

Synthèse
*Lourde* est un poème de clôture — émotionnelle, cosmique et linguistique. Le double sens du mot « épervier » reflète la double nature du fardeau : personnelle et métaphysique. Le cri façonné se substitue au cri spontané ; le scandale se referme sur le filet ; le temps scelle l'étoile ; la lourdeur devient identité. Le poème est à la fois un tombeau et un commentaire sur l'acte d'ensevelir.
 Michel Galiana’s poem "Lourde" is one of his most compressed and enigmatic texts, built on abrupt negations, mineral imagery, and a syntax that seems to collapse under its own weight.
The title itself — Lourde, feminine — already suggests a play on heaviness, burden, and perhaps even the slang lourdé (“kicked out”), hinting at a personal expulsion or emotional eviction.
The poem’s density conceals a biographical undercurrent that becomes clearer when one recalls the “young girl from Saint‑Cloud,” a figure who haunts several of Galiana’s texts from the late 1980s and early 1990s.

1. The double meaning of "épervier": net and hawk
Galiana loved wordplay and semantic bifurcation. "Épervier" can mean both a "sparrowhawk" and a "cast net". In this poem, the fishing net is undoubtedly primary: the imagery of closure, weight, and immobility fits the poem’s mineral lexicon. Yet the hawk is not entirely absent. The bird of prey — symbol of suddenness, predation, and youthful violence — evokes the morbid phantasmagoria associated with the Saint‑Cloud memory. The “distant weight of dreams and bones” is not only metaphysical; it is autobiographical. The scandale that “closes upon the net” is the old obsession returning, but now subdued, contained, immobilized.

2. From spontaneous cry to crafted cry
“Le cri s’agence.”
The cry no longer bursts forth; it organizes itself. The wound has healed enough that pain is no longer spontaneous. It must be constructed, shaped by the artifices of the man of letters. This transformation — from raw suffering to literary architecture — is itself a burden. The “new weight” replaces the old one: the emotional heaviness of youth gives way to the intellectual heaviness of memory, craft, and reflection.

3. The scandal closing upon the net
The line “Sur l’épervier s’est refermé le scandale” becomes clear once épervier is read as net. The scandal — the shock, the unresolved trauma — closes upon the net meant to catch it. The net is overwhelmed by the very burden it was supposed to contain. This is the poem’s emotional pivot: the old obsession returns, but now as a heavy, controlled, almost ritualized weight. The culprit is time, which freezes, calms, and seals. The “sun becomes a closed star,” a cosmic body that no longer radiates. The world is covered by a mantle of lead.

4. The feminine "Lourde": heaviness or eviction?
Why Lourde in the feminine?
The poem’s grammar does not require it. The choice is deliberate.
It may refer to:

La "lourde": the heavy slab, the tombstone, the weight itself.

"Lourde / lourdé": a pun suggesting expulsion, rejection, being “kicked out.”

This double resonance fits the poem’s emotional landscape: the poet is both burdened and expelled — burdened by memory, expelled from youth, expelled from the intensity of the original wound.

5. A link with Les funérailles de l’amour
Christian, your intuition is correct: "Lourde" should be read alongside Les funérailles de l’amour (1990). Both poems deal with the burial of a past passion, the transformation of emotional violence into mineral stillness, and the oppressive calm that follows the storm. In both, love becomes a weight, a slab, a sealed star.

6. Final immobility
The poem ends with two nouns: "Plomb et dalle".
Lead and slab.
No verb, no movement, no escape.
The world has become mineral.
The cry has become architecture.
The wound has become stone.

Synthesis
Lourde is a poem of closure — emotional, cosmic, and linguistic. The double meaning of épervier mirrors the double nature of the burden: personal and metaphysical. The crafted cry replaces the spontaneous one; the scandal closes upon the net; time seals the star; heaviness becomes identity. The poem is both a tomb and a commentary on the act of burying.
