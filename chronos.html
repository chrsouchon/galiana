XVI CHRONOS

Le sans - visage, sang du seul coeur issu,
Sous la clarté devenu sol, sel, usages,
Mer où surnage la splendeur d'un visage
Qui se croit roc sur un sommeil jamais su,

Bloc fissuré d'où coulent, fluent et se suc-
cèdent, an après an, rituels sages,
(Et quelquefois il se devine passage,
Cet univers, pour un craquement perçu)

Île brisée et flottaison, il et île
Enorgueilli de la croyance futile
Qu'un coquillage est le prétexte du flot,

Sol refondu de la saumure qui ronge
(Songe durci, mais la substance est de songe)
Puis dans le sang diluant son bref caillot.

7 mai 1992

XVI CHRONOS

O faceless one, pouring blood that from one's heart streams,
The sun turned you to salt, soil, reliable trace,
A sea whereon lingers the splendour of a face
That means to be a rock amidst unconscious dreams,

Cracked stone exuding in continuous flow
Year after year, rituals, appropriate and sound,
(Though, when a squeak is heard in this world's undergrounds
You may suspect, sometimes, the change they undergo).

Broken isle, floating wreck. Whether vessel or reef,
You delude yourself with the deceptive belief
That a mere shell may be the pretext for a flood,

Soil that the brine has drenched, gnawing and corroding
(A dream gone hard, though its substance remains dream still)
As has, its short-lived clot once diluted, the blood.

May 7th 1992
Transl. Christian Souchon 01.04.2006 - 2026 (c) (r) All rights reserved

Note :

La classique opposition entre temps objectif et temps subjectif. Ici c'est le temps du sol et de la mer qui s'oppose au temps du sang. A la strophe 3 cette dychotomie donne lieu aujeu de mot minimaliste: "il" et "île", parfaitement intraduisible!
A en juger par cette même strophe, Michel ne faisait pas sienne le théorie de la causalité hyperbolique (le battement d'aile d'un papillon qui déclenche un cataclisme).

 The classic opposition between objective time and subjective time. Here it is the time of soil and sea which opposes the time of blood. In stanza 3 this dichotomy gives rise to the minimalist pun: "il et île" (“he” and “island”), perfectly untranslatable!
Judging from this same stanza, Michel did not endorse the theory of hyperbolic causality (the beating of a butterfly's wing causing a hurricane).

Comments on the English translation generated by AI in 2026


Yes, I think you may certainly keep it (your English translation). In fact, rereading both texts carefully, I find that your translation captures remarkably well the poem's difficult equilibrium between cosmological vision, geological imagery, and meditation on identity and time. It is not a literal translation, but it is a poetic translation in the strongest sense: it recreates the sonnet's movement and tone in English.
A few observations.

The opening quatrain
Le sans-visage, sang du seul cœur issu,
This is extraordinarily compressed French. Literally:
"The faceless one, blood issued from the sole heart"
or
"The faceless, blood sprung from the only heart."
Your rendering:
O faceless one, pouring blood that from one's heart streams,
is freer, but effective. What is lost is the idea that the "blood" may itself be the faceless being, a kind of emanation from the primordial heart. What is gained is fluency and rhythmic ease.
The most debatable choice here is probably:
from one's heart
for
du seul cœur
since "seul" is "sole", "single", "only", not an indefinite "one's". Yet I understand why you chose it: "only heart" sounds rather awkward in English verse.

The next lines:
Sous la clarté devenu sol, sel, usages,
Mer où surnage la splendeur d'un visage
are admirably condensed in French through the alliterative Your:
The sun turned you to salt, soil, reliable trace,
A sea whereon lingers the splendour of a face
succeeds in preserving the accumulation of transformations.
"Reliable trace" is not literally usages, but it evokes sedimented habit and permanence.
One feels the effort to recreate rather than merely translate.

The second quatrain
I particularly like:
Cracked stone exuding in continuous flow
for Bloc fissuré d'où coulent, fluent et se suc- cèdent
[AI failed to notice the caesura that isolates the word "suc"="juice"!]
The original's triple movement:
• coulent
• fluent
• se succèdent
is almost untranslatable. Galiana deliberately multiplies synonyms of flowing and succeeding. English cannot reproduce the effect exactly.
Your solution sacrifices the verbal play but preserves the sense of duration.

The parenthesis:
(Et quelquefois il se devine passage, Cet univers...)
is subtly rendered:
You may suspect, sometimes, the change they undergo.
Here, however, something shifts. The French says not that things change, but that the universe itself may be guessed to be a passage, a transit, a crossing-place. This is one of the poem's deepest insights. The cosmos is not a structure but a movement. If revising today, I might seek a way to retain passage, for example:
You may perceive at times this universe as passage
Yet your version remains perfectly defensible.

 The sestet
The most striking achievement, to my ear, lies in:
Broken isle, floating wreck. Whether vessel or reef,
You delude yourself with the deceptive belief.
This is a genuine recreation.
The French:
Île brisée et flottaison, il et île
contains an untranslatable pun:
• île (island); il (he)
The self is both "he" and "island".
You rightly abandon the impossible wordplay and construct another duality: vessel or reef
which retains the uncertainty of identity.
Similarly
: Qu'un coquillage est le prétexte du flot
is rendered as
That a mere shell may be the pretext for a flood
A very intelligent choice. "Flood" amplifies the energy of flot while preserving the paradox of cause and effect.

The closing tercet
The French conclusion is magnificent:
Sol refondu de la saumure qui ronge
(Songe durci, mais la substance est de songe)
Puis dans le sang diluant son bref caillot.
The whole sonnet has been moving toward dissolution.
• rock becomes fissure,
• island becomes drift,
• solid ground becomes brine-soaked matter,
• dream hardens,
• hardness returns to dream,
• clot dissolves back into blood.
Chronos is therefore not merely time. He is the universal solvent.
Your:
Hardened dream whose substance itself is dream bygone
is perhaps the least successful line, though still serviceable.
The French says something subtler:
"a dream hardened, but whose substance remains dream."
Galiana's paradox is ontological rather than temporal. The dream is not bygone; it merely appears solid.
If I were tinkering today, I might suggest:
A dream gone hard, though its substance is still a dream
But this is revision at a very fine level.

Overall assessment
What strikes me most is that your translation already reflects an excellent understanding of the poem's architecture.
The sonnet describes a succession of mistaken stabilizations:
1. the faceless becoming a face;
2. the flowing becoming a rock;
3. the passage becoming a universe;
4. the sea becoming an island;
5. the dream becoming substance;
6. the clot becoming identity.
Chronos patiently reverses each illusion.
The final image of the clot returning to blood mirrors the opening image of blood issuing from the primordial heart. The poem therefore forms a kind of cycle: individuation emerges from a greater flow and eventually dissolves back into it.

Your translation preserves that cyclical movement. It occasionally relaxes Galiana's metaphysical density, but it never betrays it. For a translation done in 2006, intended for publication, I would certainly preserve it, perhaps revising only three places if you wished:
• du seul cœur → retain the notion of the "single" or "sole" heart;
• cet univers... passage → preserve the idea of the universe as passage rather than change;
• la substance est de songe → retain the ontological paradox that the substance remains dream.
Otherwise, it reads as the work of someone who had already grasped what Galiana was doing: expressing, through geological and maritime metaphors, the ancient intuition that all forms are transient coagulations in a deeper stream of being. That seems to me exactly the metaphysic of Chronos underlying the sonnet.
